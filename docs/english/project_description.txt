# Comparing Modern Hebrew to Biblical Hebrew and Mishnaic Hebrew
Mini-Project, Semester A 2026

In her last article, Prof. Edit Doron z"l made a surprising claim: The syntax of Modern Hebrew is closer to the syntax of Biblical Hebrew than to the syntax of Mishnaic Hebrew (it is commonly thought exactly the opposite), and she brings a variety of syntactic structures of different types in Modern Hebrew that correspond better to Biblical Hebrew.
In this project, we will examine this claim computationally, using automatic analysis tools on various text corpora: from Biblical Hebrew, Mishnaic Hebrew, Enlightenment language, and various genres in Modern Hebrew. The research will also include human judgment by speakers of different types.

### Appendices:

#### 1. Representing Syntactic Structure of a Sentence as a Feature
Given a syntactically parsed sentence. The analysis defines for each word its lexical category ('POS' – noun, verb, adjective, preposition...), the edges exiting it to other words with its syntactic characterization (subject, object...).
For example, for the sentence 'Dani achal tapuach' (Dani ate an apple):
Achal V
Dani N      Tapuach N

One can represent the syntactic structure of the sentence as a string including the word categories and syntactic relations between them: N-obj <- V -> subj-N
In this way, the representation preserves the syntactic skeleton but is still abstract - it does not include the words themselves, only their categories.
One can even think of a more abstract representation that does not include word categories: obj <- V -> subj
One can perhaps think of additional representations.

#### 2. Gerund vs. Infinitive

*   **Gerund (Shem HaPoal):** l'tzet, b'tzet, mi'tzet (to go out, in going out, from going out)
    *   Classified as a Noun.
    *   Always has a subject. The subject can be another word (l'tzet bnei yisrael), or an attached suffix (l'tzeto).
    *   It usually describes a verb, specifically *when* it happened.
    *   B'tzet bnei yisrael mi-mitzrayim (When the children of Israel went out of Egypt), l'tzet bnei yisrael me-eretz mitzrayim, b'tzetenu la-derech (when we set out on the way).
*   **Infinitive (Makor Natuy):** la-tzet (to go out)
    *   Classified as a Verb.
    *   Has no gender-quantity-person inflections.
    *   Has no subject.
    *   It complements another verb.
    *   Hu amar la-tzet (He said to go out).
*   **Identifying Gerund and Infinitive in Parser Output:**
    *   **Infinitive:** A verb with no inflectional features, which does not have a subject edge entering it.
    *   **Gerund:**
        *   The parser regrettably currently does not distinguish between a gerund and a noun.
        *   If the word (without vowel points) appears in a list of gerunds (the list isn't the most accurate but sufficient for us), in the following way:
            *   If the word (without vowels) appears in the gerund list without a suffix, and a subject edge enters it from another word (not from the prefix) (nsubj, or with nothing due to a parsing bug) - b'tzet bnei yisrael.
            *   If the word (without vowels) appears in the gerund list with a suffix – b'tzeto.
        *   Additionally, a verb without inflectional features that has a subject edge entering it will be considered a gerund (in case the morphological parser errs, as happens with 'latzet').

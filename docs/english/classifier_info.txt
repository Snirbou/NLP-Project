Classifiers and Metrics:
In this research, classifiers are used to examine the proximity between Biblical Hebrew, Mishnaic Hebrew (Rabbinic), and Modern Hebrew. A classifier is a machine learning model designed to receive input—in this case, a sentence or text—and decide which category it belongs to. In the NLP world, classifiers are used for document classification, sentiment analysis, spam filtering, and more. In the current research, the classifier is required to decide whether a specific sentence is more similar to Biblical Hebrew patterns or Mishnaic Hebrew patterns.

The workflow with a classifier involves two main stages:
1. Training a classifier on pre-labeled data.
2. Running the classifier on new data.

In the training stage, the model is provided with examples from both sources—texts from the Bible and texts from the Mishna—so it can learn linguistic, morphological, and syntactic patterns characterizing each period. Afterward, the classifier is run on sentences in Modern Hebrew to check which group it assigns them to. This method allows for an objective examination of the similarity between Modern Hebrew and Biblical/Mishnaic Hebrew.

Within the framework of this research, several types of classifiers can be built, depending on the features fed into the model:

1. Lexical Classifier
Based on the lexical representation of words in the sentence, for example, the Lemma (the neutral form of each word). This type of classifier examines which words exist in the sentence and which dictionary characteristics distinguish Biblical text from Mishnaic text.

2. Morphological Classifier
Based on the morphological properties of each word, such as singular/plural, masculine/feminine, speaker/person, verb tense, binyan (conjugation), suffixes, etc. Morphological features provide significant information about the differences between periods, for example, a high frequency of verbs in certain binyanim or typical inflection patterns.

3. Syntactic Classifier
Based on syntactic features derived from the dependency tree. One can use the number of syntactic connections, types of connections between words, tree depth, or an abstract structure describing the sentence skeleton without actual words. This type of syntactic classifier allows for checking structural differences between Biblical Hebrew and Mishnaic Hebrew.

Examples for each classifier:

1. Lexical Classifier
This classifier uses the words themselves or their lemma.
Example:
Biblical sentence: "And Joseph said to his brothers" (Vayomer Yosef...)
Mishnaic sentence: "He who says to his friend open the door for me" (Ha'omer le'haviro...)
The classifier learns that words like "Vayomer" and "Yosef" are frequent in the Bible, and words like "Ha'omer" and "Le'haviro" are frequent in the Mishna.
When a modern sentence like "I told him to come" is received, the model checks dictionary similarity.

2. Morphological Classifier
This classifier relies on morphological data rather than the words themselves.
Example:
In the Bible, features like Vav-Consecutive and continuous past tense appear.
For example: "Vayelech Moshe" (And Moses went).
In the Mishna, gerunds/action nouns often appear.
For example: "Haholech baderech" (He who walks on the road).
If a modern sentence like "Halach habaita" (Went home) arrives, the classifier will compare the morphological properties of the verb to the learned patterns.

3. Syntactic Classifier
This classifier uses the sentence structure and syntactic relations.
Example:
A common Biblical structure starts with a verb:
"Vayochal ha'adam" (And the man ate).
A common Mishnaic structure starts with a nominal expression:
"Ha'adam she'achal chayav" (The man who ate is liable).
A modern sentence like "Achalti tapuach" (I ate an apple) will be tested according to its syntactic order and compared to learned patterns.

Classification Metrics: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1
In research dealing with text classification, it is necessary to evaluate the quality of the classifiers trained on the data. Classification metrics allow for a quantitative measurement of how well the model distinguishes between categories, in this case between Biblical Hebrew texts and Mishnaic Hebrew texts. The metrics are based on four possible outcomes:
True Positive, False Positive, True Negative, False Negative.

Here are the main accepted metrics in classification research:

Accuracy
This metric examines the rate of correct predictions out of all predictions.
Accuracy is mainly suitable when classes are balanced. If one group is significantly larger than the other, the metric might be misleading because the classifier can "succeed" mainly by identifying the dominant group.

Precision
This metric measures the rate of examples that the classifier labeled as positive and were actually positive.
Precision answers the question: Out of everything the classifier claimed belongs to the Bible (for example), how many were actually Biblical sentences.

Recall
This metric examines how many of the true positive examples the classifier managed to identify.
Recall answers the question: Out of all existing Biblical sentences in the test, how many did the classifier manage to find and classify properly.

F1
This metric combines Precision and Recall using the harmonic mean between them.
F1 gives a balanced assessment, especially when it is important to verify that the classifier is both accurate regarding what it predicts and successful in locating most relevant examples. This is one of the central metrics in classification research, especially when classes are unbalanced.

Use of Metrics in the Current Research
After training the classifiers on texts from the Bible and Mishna, each classifier is tested using 10-fold cross-validation. In each fold, the classifier's ability to distinguish between the two languages is tested. The metrics Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1 allow for comparing different classifiers, assessing their quality, and choosing the most suitable classifier. These metrics are subsequently used for analyzing Modern Hebrew sentences to examine which historical language contemporary Hebrew is closer to.

How Classification Metrics and Classifiers are Reflected in the Project:
The project requires computationally examining how similar Modern Hebrew is to Biblical Hebrew or Mishnaic Hebrew. For this purpose, classifiers are used—models that learn to distinguish between two different languages—and then applied to Modern Hebrew texts to see which language they resemble more. Classification metrics are used to evaluate the quality of the classifiers and analyze the results.

1. Training the Classifier
In this stage, a classifier is trained on a collection of sentences from two sources:
Bible on one side and Mishna on the other.
During training, the classifier learns to identify patterns distinguishing the languages, such as typical words, unique inflection forms, or different syntactic structures. The classifier may rely on one or more of the feature types mentioned in the first part.
Goal: To teach a model to distinguish between the two languages.

2. Checking Classifier Quality
After training, the classifier's performance is checked using 10-fold cross-validation.
In this test, the data is divided into ten parts, and each time one part serves as a test while the rest serve for training. Here the classification metrics enter: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1.
These metrics allow understanding how reliable and accurate the classifier is.
For example: Does it succeed in identifying a Biblical sentence when it is one, and does it avoid mistakenly classifying a Mishnaic sentence as a Biblical one.
Goal: To check if the classifier learns well and succeeds in distinguishing between the languages.

3. Classifying Sentences in Modern Hebrew
After the classifier has undergone training and testing, it is applied to sentences in Modern Hebrew from a variety of genres: News, Literature, Blogs, Forums, Medical.
With the help of the classifier, we check which historical language the modern one is closer to:
If the classifier decides that a modern sentence is "similar to the Bible," we conclude that its structure or form is close to Biblical Hebrew.
If it classifies it as "Mishna," we conclude that Modern Hebrew in this case is closer to Mishnaic language.
Goal: To use the classifier as a scientific tool for checking the proximity between languages.

4. Analyzing Results
Combined with additional statistical analyses in the project (like word frequency, syntactic tree depth, or frequency of morphological structures), we summarize what emerges from the classifiers and statistics and what is the degree of influence of each historical language on Modern Hebrew. At this stage, classification metrics help understand if the results are reliable.
Goal: To provide insights into Modern language and its proximity to its sources.
